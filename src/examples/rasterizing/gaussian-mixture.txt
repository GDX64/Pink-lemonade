Abstract—Driven by advances in supercomputing, the scale
of scientific simulation data has grown dramatically. In fields
such as cosmology, particle data have become a common representation, with state-of-the-art simulations now exceeding the
trillion-particle mark. Consequently, the challenge of visually
analyzing such massive datasets has become increasingly urgent.
The traditional visual analysis workflow typically follows a
”compression → storage → reconstruction → visualization”
pipeline. However, this process is hampered by an extremely timeconsuming reconstruction stage, which severely impedes real-time
interactive visualization. Moreover, in multi-time-step analyses,
the enormous volume of reconstructed data creates significant I/O
bottlenecks. In this work, we draw inspiration from 3D Gaussian
splatting and compress the simulation data using Gaussian
Mixture Models (GMMs), treating the resulting Gaussian kernels as fundamental rendering primitives. Our method renders
billion-scale particles for each timestep in approximately 32 ms,
requiring only 645 MB of GPU memory per timestep — nearly
20× smaller than the original 12 GB raw data. This eliminates
costly reconstruction, accelerates the visual analysis pipeline, and
overcomes I/O bottlenecks in multi-time-step analysis. Extensive
experiments and comparisons across multiple datasets validate
the effectiveness of our method.
Index Terms—Particle rendering, Gaussian mixture model,
Data compression.
I. INTRODUCTION
With the rapid advancement of supercomputing capabilities, the scale and complexity of scientific simulations have
increased dramatically. Consequently, the data generated by
scientific simulations have become significantly larger and
more intricate. Particle data, in particular, is a fundamental
representation widely employed in various scientific domains,
including cosmology, meteorology, fluid dynamics, etc. Notably, cosmological numerical simulations often involve particle datasets comprising billions or even trillions of particles,
resulting in enormous data volumes. For example, in the
commonly used cosmological N-body [1] simulation, the data
generated in a single time step using 10243 particles can
reach 32 GB. If multi-time-step data are analyzed without

quickly become prohibitive, making it difficult to handle long
temporal sequences of the simulation in practice. In particular,
these data comprise only billion-level particles, while modern
supercomputers can generate datasets with even larger particle
counts. This imposes substantial demands on the efficiency
and effectiveness of data storage, visualization, and analysis

Currently, the visual analysis of large-scale particle data typically follows a ”compression → storage → reconstruction →
visualization” workflow, as it is impractical to store raw data
directly due to extreme I/O pressures from supercomputerbased simulations. Consequently, an in-situ compression strategy is employed—where data is compressed during computation on the supercomputer before storage—followed by subsequent data reconstruction for post-hoc analysis. This solution effectively mitigates storage and bandwidth requirements
while maintaining data accessibility for visualization and analysis. When implementing compression for such extreme-scale
simulations, researchers typically prioritize lossy approaches
over lossless methods. This preference stems from the fundamental limitation of lossless techniques (e.g., Huffman coding
[2] and LZW [3]) - while they preserve all information perfectly, their compression ratios are often insufficient to address
the massive I/O demands. In contrast, well-designed lossy
compression (e.g., SZ [4]) can achieve significantly higher ratios while maintaining scientific validity. In our previous work
[5], we employed the Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) for
in-situ compression of particle data, effectively modeling the
data distribution to preserve the most representative features
and achieving a 100× compression ratio, thereby significantly
reducing the storage burden.
However, the visual analysis process based on the “compression → storage → reconstruction → visualization” workflow
still suffers from two major issues. First, the reconstruction process is exceedingly time-consuming and computationally intensive, posing a major bottleneck that
substantially hinders the subsequent visualization and
analysis workflows of large-scale particle datasets. Compressed data—whether obtained through lossless or lossy
methods—cannot be used directly, as it is typically a highly
abstracted, low-dimensional representation that requires complex decoding, inverse transformations, or interpolation to

reconstruct data approximating its original form. This reconstruction process is extremely time-consuming. For example, the SZ algorithm necessitates predictive error estimation
and rigorous error inversion procedures to ensure that the
reconstructed data meets predefined accuracy requirements,
while our previous work based on GMMs requires extensive
sampling and probability density estimation to approximate
the original data distribution. These additional computational
steps not only introduce significant delays into the overall
workflow but also easily become bottlenecks when processing
massive datasets, thus impeding real-time analysis. Second,
the traditional processing mode requires complete particle data reconstruction at each timestep, creating severe
memory-bound limitations where the iterative reconstruction process not only incurs prohibitively high memory
footprints that routinely exceed available memory budgets,
but also inevitably produces substantial redundant I/O
operations. For instance, in the aforementioned simulation
involving billion-level particles, analyzing even about 100 time
steps would already require a data volume on the order of
several terabytes, which still poses a substantial challenge for
current computational systems.
To address the issues mentioned above, we build upon
our previous work and further streamline the original visual
analysis workflow. Inspired by 3D Gaussian splatting [6]—an
emerging technique that represents scenes with Gaussian
primitives for efficient reconstruction and rendering—we treat
each Gaussian component in our GMM-compressed particle
representation as a fundamental splatting primitive. Beyond
density, our method can jointly model additional particle
attributes and support their visualization within the same
Gaussian-based representation. Leveraging the mathematical
properties of Gaussians, we design view transformation operations tailored to these primitives, enabling flexible control of visualization appearance through a set of rendering
parameters. To maximize efficiency, we develop a CUDAparallel rendering pipeline that bypasses costly samplingbased reconstruction. This design greatly speeds up the overall
workflow and supports real-time, interactive exploration. In
addition, by rendering directly from the compressed representation, we markedly lower the memory requirements for
large-scale visualization, making multi-time-step time-series
analysis more practical and efficient. In previous research,
Rapp et al. [7] introduced a similar strategy called ”Backto-Front Splatting” that also rendered particle data directly on
GMM’s Gaussian kernels using ray integration. However, this
method is inherently less efficient than our splatting approach.
Although their use of an approximate closed-form integral
made it a splatting-like rendering and sped up the rendering
process, it came at the cost of visual quality. In summary, the
contributions of this work are as follows:
• We propose a novel visualization pipeline for largescale particle simulation, which bypasses the original
costly reconstruction step by introducing our GMM-based
splatting for direct rendering of compressed data.
• We achieve state-of-the-art performance in cosmological
particle rendering, delivering superior visual quality while
maintaining exceptional rendering efficiency.
• We validate the memory efficiency and practical applicability of our method by successfully processing largescale cosmological simulations with up to billion-level
particle counts, and enabling temporal analysis over hundreds of time steps with an overall data volume of several
terabytes.
II. RELATED WORK
In this section, we discuss previous works on particle rendering and Gaussian-based data representation and rendering.
A. Particle Rendering
Our work is closely related to particle rendering, for which
various methods have been developed, each with its own
advantages and disadvantages. Among these techniques, the
most intuitive and flexible is the rasterization of point primitives or point sprites [8]–[11]. This approach is universally
applicable to all types of particle data and benefits from
hardware acceleration. Its core concept involves directly rasterizing each particle onto a fixed grid of pixels on the screen
[12]. However, the inherently discrete nature of particle data
can lead to the appearance of numerous gaps—unrendered
empty pixels between disconnected points—in the final image,
a shortcoming for applications that require smooth surfaces,
such as smoke [13] or fire [14]. To address this issue, postrendering filtering is commonly applied [9], [15], [16]. In the
visualization of cosmological particle simulation data—such
as N-body simulations [1] or cosmological SPH simulations
[17]—these gaps are often acceptable. Nonetheless, further
improvements have been introduced; for instance, Dubinski et
al. [18] transform each particle into a blob defined by a spread
function, thereby producing a more realistic rendering effect.
Currently, this method is still commonly used in cosmological
particle visualization [5], [19], so we adopt this method as the
ground truth for our work.
Alternative approaches, including splatting [20], [21] and
3D glyphs [22], [23], address the gap issue prior to rendering.
Splatting involves rendering individual points as view- or
surface-aligned billboards or disks with a specified extent in
world space. These splats are typically textured with Gaussian
intensity profiles and subsequently blended in image space
[24]. Although this method requires the estimation of several
parameters for each splat, resulting in additional storage overhead, the 3D glyphs approach offers a computationally more
efficient solution by rendering each point as a sphere [25]
or paraboloid [26], thereby mitigating the gap problem while
reducing the computational load.
Ray tracing methods [27] also offer a pathway to highquality particle rendering. However, the challenge of performing ray intersections with point primitives necessitates
either converting these primitives into alternative forms—such
as polynomial surfaces [28] or spheres [29]—or employing
cones or cylinders for density-based tracing [30], [31]. More
recently, neural network-based particle rendering approaches
have emerged. These techniques typically apply conventional
JOURNAL OF LATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021 3
rendering methods as a first step, followed by hole-filling or
photorealistic shading via a neural network [32], [33].
Despite these advances, the methods described above still
struggle with extremely large-scale particle datasets, such
as those containing billions of particles. As a result, many
efforts focus on reducing data size and access cost through
compression and specialized data structures. In the context
of cosmological particle simulations, a variety of lossless
compressors—such as BloscLZ [34], LZ4 [35], and Zstd
[36]—and lossy schemes—such as FPZIP [37], ZFP [38], and
SZ3 [39]—have been explored. While these methods provide
good rate-distortion performance, they typically achieve compression ratios of at most about an order of magnitude, which
remains insufficient for truly large-scale particle rendering.
Complementary to compression, Wald et al. [40] propose a
memory-efficient balanced p-k-d tree acceleration structure
and integrate it into the OSPRay framework for full-detail ray
tracing of billion-particle datasets. Gralka et al. [41] introduce
a two-level nested acceleration structure with a hardwareaccelerated bounding volume hierarchies outer layer and pk-d trees or flat particle lists as inner nodes, and demonstrate
improved memory-efficient interactive ray tracing for large 3D
particle data. In our work, k-d tree partitioning likewise serves
as a key preprocessing step for organizing the data before
GMM-based compression and rendering.
B. Gaussian-based Data Representation and Rendering
The Gaussian distribution is fundamental to data representation and rendering. In large-scale data processing, it serves
both as a reasonable approximation for natural and social
phenomena and as an effective model for data noise and error.
The Gaussian mixture model [42] is a vital data representation tool. For instance, Liu et al. [43] employ per-voxel
GMMs to model volumetric data for uncertainty visualization.
Athawale et al. [44] further extend this line of work by
incorporating nonparametric uncertainty modeling into direct
volume rendering, enabling more flexible probabilistic representations beyond parametric GMMs. Similarly, Dutta et al.
[45] and Li et al. [46] use GMMs in situ as a data proxy
to extract the most salient features from the data, thereby
alleviating I/O overhead and reducing storage requirements;
notably, the latter work represents our previous efforts in
particle data compression. Another important model is the
Gaussian process, which has been applied by Grosskopf et al.
[47] and Li et al. [19] to model various scientific simulation
datasets.
Beyond their role in data representation, Gaussian models can also serve as primitives for data rendering [48],
[49]. Specifically, Zwicker et al. [50] discuss the splatting
of elliptical Gaussians by approximating their footprint after
perspective projection. Rapp et al. [7] derive a closed-form
integral for the Gaussian kernels of GMMs to facilitate fast
rendering of multivariate scattered data—a method that closely
resembles our work and serves as our comparison baseline.
Recently, 3D Gaussian splatting [6] has emerged as a predominant technique in 3D scene reconstruction, garnering significant attention. This method represents scenes using Gaussian
kernels endowed with opacity and color, and leverages a
tile-based parallel strategy for rapid rasterization, achieving
high efficiency and rendering quality that far exceeds NeRFbased methods [51]. With continued advancements, Gaussian
splatting has matured into a reliable rendering approach, and
its fast rasterization algorithm based on Gaussian models has
become a baseline for 3D object reconstruction and rendering.
Inspired by Gaussian splatting, we first compress particle
data using GMMs, and then treat each Gaussian component as
the basic rendering primitive for splatting-based visualization
to enable the visual analysis of large-scale particle datasets.
III. BACKGROUND
In the conventional large-scale particle visual analysis workflow—“compression → storage → reconstruction → visualization”—we adopt a GMM-based splatting technique to bypass
the time-consuming reconstruction stage and achieve direct
rendering. In this section, we provide a detailed exposition of
the mathematical background of splatting: first, the original
splatting concept [52] is introduced, followed by an exploration of the 3D Gaussian [6] splatting designed for realistic
3D scene reconstruction and rendering, thereby laying the
theoretical foundation for subsequent implementations.
A. Basic Splatting
The original formulation of splatting [52] introduced a
rendering paradigm fundamentally different from ray casting,
and in retrospect can be seen as laying the groundwork for
subsequent developments. In ray casting, the final image is
produced by calculating the contribution of each voxel to
every pixel. In contrast, splatting computes how each voxel
affects every pixel by treating each voxel as a blurred spatial
sphere, referred to as the reconstruction kernel. Voxels from
different regions are splatted onto different image planes, and
the rendered image is obtained by fusing these planes via alpha
blending. The specific rendering details are as follows:
First, the volume is traversed and discretized into multiple
image planes parallel to the final projection plane, and each
voxel is assigned to its nearest plane—a strategy we similarly
adopt in our method. The voxel’s grid coordinates (i, j, k) are
then transformed to screen-space coordinates (Dx, Dy, Dz).
Assuming an orthographic projection along the z-axis, the
contribution of a voxel projected onto one image plane can
be written as

IV. METHOD
To overcome the time-consuming data reconstruction required by traditional methods, we propose our GMM-based
particle splatting approach. Figure 1 illustrates our workflow.
Initially, for the large-scale particle simulation data, we partition the entire spatial domain using a k-d tree, dividing the
particles into smaller regions with roughly equal numbers.
We then fit a GMM to the particles in each leaf node to
obtain a compressed representation, and store the associated
parameters on disk. This GMM fitting can be performed in situ
on a supercomputer or post-simulation. During the subsequent
visualization phase, instead of resampling the learned GMM
to reconstruct the original data, we directly perform data
transformations such as translation and rotation on the GMM
parameters to map the Gaussian kernels into the camera’s view.
We then regulate the rendering size of each Gaussian kernel
using the 3σ rule, and employ our GMM-based splatting algorithm to render the particle data. This process supports both
real-time interactive analysis and multi-time-step analysis. The
details of our method are as follows.
Building on this pipeline and following our previous work
[5], we model the particle distribution within each leaf node

E. Parallel Acceleration
To enhance rendering efficiency, we employ parallel computing techniques to accelerate the entire rendering pipeline.
This pipeline is decomposed into two primary stages: the first
stage computes the normalization factors for each Gaussian
distribution, while the second stage calculates the final density
values for each pixel. Although partial redundant computations
exist between the two stages, considering that each Gaussian
distribution may influence thousands of pixels, the memory
overhead for storing intermediate variables outweighs the cost
of direct recomputation.
In the CUDA implementation, a parallel strategy of ”one
thread block per Gaussian distribution” is adopted. Multiple
threads within each thread block concurrently process pixel
calculations corresponding to the same Gaussian distribution.
This design fully exploits the computational homogeneity of
pixel evaluations within a Gaussian, which naturally lends
itself to multithreaded parallel processing. The accumulated
results are stored in a buffer allocated in device global memory,
and the contributions from all threads in the block are combined using global-memory atomic additions. In this way, we
ensure both the correctness of the normalization coefficients
and the avoidance of data races, while achieving a significant
improvement in computational efficiency.

on synthetic data. Next, we evaluate our method from both

In this section, we present the datasets, baselines, evaluation

[56] and Illustris-3 [57]. The following provides a detailed
overview of these data sources.
• The synthetic data are constructed using GMMs with
predefined mean, covariance, and weight parameters for
controlled evaluation. We generated particle samples from
GMMs with varying component numbers and particle

VI. DISCUSSION
In this section, we discuss the reasons why Gaussian splatting cannot be directly employed for particle rendering and
compare our method with prior splatting techniques. We also
discuss the scalability and limitations of our method.
A. The Reasons Why Gaussian Splatting Cannot be Directly
Employed
As one of the most popular methods for 3D reconstruction
currently, 3D Gaussian splatting [6] has significant efficiency
advantages and flexibility in the representation and rendering
of real-world scenes. With in-depth research in this area, both
its rendering effect and efficiency have been considerably
improved, and its application scenarios are also very extensive.
However, Gaussian splatting still cannot be directly employed
for particle rendering because it has fundamental differences
from particle rendering in terms of coloring methods, data
scale, and rendering processes.
First, in Gaussian splatting, colors are defined using spherical harmonic functions. A set of functions on the sphere
is defined through 48 parameters of the third-order spherical harmonic functions, which, respectively, define the RGB
color values on the Gaussian surface. These color values are
then used directly in subsequent rendering. However, in the
rendering of cosmological particle data, the colors of the final
rendered images are determined by the particle density. A prespecified transfer function is used to convert the density into
colors, which facilitates the observation of experts in relevant
fields. Therefore, it is not feasible to define the colors of the
final rendered images using Gaussian splatting’s approach.
Second, during the rendering process, Gaussian splatting
directly considers the entire space. After sorting by height, the
occlusion relationship between Gaussians is handled through
alpha-blending. However, for particle rendering, it is neither
realistic nor necessary to sort and perform alpha-blending
on such large-scale data. In our approach, the entire particle
space is first divided into N image planes. After projecting
nearby Gaussians onto the nearest plane, multiple images are
composited via alpha-blending.
Finally, Gaussian splatting is an iterative optimization process that requires multiple rounds of multi-view rendering.
In each iteration, rendered images are compared with precaptured multi-view reference images to compute the loss,
which is then backpropagated to update parameters. As a
result, multiple rendering passes are needed to gradually refine
the model. However, in practical large-scale particle rendering
tasks, it is often unrealistic to prepare such reference images
due to the lack of fast rendering algorithms. Therefore, the
final image must be generated efficiently in a single rendering
pass.
B. Comparison with Prior Splatting Techniques
Both B2FS and our method perform rendering directly on
the compressed GMM data to obtain the final rendered image,
but they fundamentally differ in their underlying rendering
logic and visual outcomes.
B2FS employs a traditional approach by integrating translucent Gaussian kernels, which is closely related to ray tracing.
In essence, this rendering method computes the integral of
light contributions from all Gaussians along each pixel’s ray
path. In contrast, splatting-based methods determine which
pixels each primitive covers, offering inherent efficiency advantages in rendering. To improve performance, B2FS derives
a closed-form solution for infinite integrals, transforming its
rendering into a splatting-like process that achieves efficiency
comparable to ours. However, this comes at the cost of
rendering quality.
By extending the original finite integral to an infinite domain
for a closed-form solution, B2FS tends to overestimate regions
with originally low values. Additionally, the integral results
per pixel are typically very small (far less than 1), requiring
rescaling to match the ground truth’s numerical range. This
rescaling often obscures details in darker regions, leading to a
visually dimmer output. Moreover, since real-world rendering
scenarios lack direct access to ground truth, rescaling the
integral results becomes impractical. In contrast, our method
can directly share the transfer function with the ground truth,
ensuring both rendering efficiency and enabling domain experts to observe and analyze the results without additional
processing.
Classical splatting techniques, such as Westover [52] and
EWA volume splatting [50], are primarily designed for volume
rendering of structured grid data, where each voxel sample
is treated as a Gaussian kernel centered at grid locations
to reconstruct a continuous scalar field through smoothing.
In contrast, our method operates directly on discrete particle
data, where each Gaussian represents a local particle density
distribution and its associated attributes rather than a voxelbased smoothing kernel.
Moreover, traditional splatting methods perform projection
via path-integral formulations and rely on footprint tables to
approximate screen-space contributions. In our framework, the
projection of a 3D Gaussian is computed analytically through
matrix transformation, directly yielding its 2D projected parameters without footprint construction, thereby improving
efficiency and reducing approximation errors.
Finally, footprint-based splatting is most effective for radially symmetric kernels. However, the Gaussians arising in
cosmological particle modeling are typically anisotropic. Footprint approximations in such cases can introduce additional
blurring, whereas our analytic projection preserves anisotropic
structures more faithfully.
C. Scalability of Our Method
In our current experiments, the largest dataset processed
is billion-scale simulated data. While modern supercomputers
can handle data far exceeding this scale, it may pose challenges
when handling scenarios with even larger datasets or more
time steps.
Let M denote the total number of particles. In practical
training, if the covariance matrix of each Gaussian in the
GMM can be well approximated by a diagonal matrix, only
its diagonal elements are used; if the model fails to converge,

the full covariance matrix is employed. Consequently, the
number of parameters stored for each GMM is approximately
between 13 and 19. To achieve a 1% compression rate, the
final number of Gaussians can be inferred to be approximately
between 3M
950 and 3M
650 . For a fixed resolution, since the 3σ
ellipsoidal scale corresponding to each Gaussian varies, the
number of affected pixels also varies. Let L denote the average
number of pixels influenced by a single Gaussian. Therefore, the overall algorithm needs to process approximately
O(0.04ML) pixels. In an ideal parallel implementation, each
Gaussian’s corresponding pixels are processed by a single
thread block. However, the total number of Gaussians typically
exceeds the available number of thread blocks (denoted by
T), which means each thread block must sequentially process
the pixel regions of multiple Gaussians. Assuming that the
computation per pixel is constant, the parallel time complexity
is approximately O(0.04ML/T). Since both 0.04 and T
are constants, this complexity is asymptotically equivalent
to O(ML). Therefore, the time complexity of the algorithm
is primarily determined by the total number of particles M
and the resolution (as represented by L), leading to a linear
increase in computation with increases in either parameter.
In terms of space complexity, we consider two aspects:
storing Gaussian parameters and pixel data. Each Gaussian
stores a constant number of parameters, and after compression,
the total number of Gaussians is linearly proportional to the
total number of particles M. Thus, this part requires O(M)
storage. Additionally, since each Gaussian affects an average
of L pixels and under a fixed resolution L is treated as a
constant, the storage for pixel-related data is also O(M). In
summary, the overall space complexity of the algorithm is
O(M).
Given the linear scaling of the algorithm with particle count
under fixed resolution, our method exhibits good scalability.
D. Limitations and Future Work
Although our method enhances the efficiency of rendering
while maintaining high quality, achieving approximately 30
fps at 1K resolution when rendering billion-level particles, it
still exhibits several limitations.
First, our approach does not yet include dedicated optimization for multi-time-step rendering. Thanks to the GMM-based
compression, each time step occupies only a small fraction of
the original memory footprint, which already allows multiple
time steps to be loaded into memory simultaneously and supports reasonably efficient temporal exploration. However, we
have not specifically optimized for temporal coherence (e.g.,
time-aware data structures or streaming strategies tailored to
long sequences). In future work, we plan to design temporal
optimizations that more explicitly exploit correlations across
time steps to further accelerate multi-time-step analysis.
Second, although our method enables direct high-resolution
image rendering, it remains insufficient for real-time multitime-step analysis at resolutions as high as 4K when dealing
with datasets containing billions of particles. Future efforts
will focus on the development of more efficient parallel
algorithms to ensure robust rendering performance even under
high-resolution conditions.
Finally, our current evaluation is restricted to cosmological
simulations, where particles serve as the primary data carrier. Many other domains—such as combustion, spray, and
other multiphase flows—also generate large-scale particlebased datasets with different spatial and attribute characteristics. Extending and adapting our GMM-based particle splatting
framework to these scenarios is an important direction for
future work, with the goal of making the method applicable
to a broader range of scientific data.
VII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose a novel visual analysis pipeline
for large-scale particle simulations that bypasses the original
costly reconstruction step by introducing our GMM-based
splatting for direct rendering of compressed particle data. We
treat each Gaussian component as the basic rendering primitive and accelerate the rendering process via parallel computing. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method
achieves state-of-the-art performance for cosmological particle
rendering, delivering superior visual quality while maintaining exceptional rendering efficiency. In addition, our method
effectively alleviates the I/O bottleneck, enabling real-time
interactive analysis and efficient multi-time-step visualization,
empowering domain experts to conduct faster and more indepth exploration of cosmological phenomena.
